                                                I. Introduction

T   he International Space Station (ISS) Water Recovery and Management (WRM) System ensures the availability
    of potable water for crew drinking and hygiene, oxygen generation, urinal flush water, and payloads as required.
To support this function, wastewater is collected in the form of crew urine, humidity condensate, and Sabatier product
water, and subsequently processed by the Water Recovery System (WRS) into potable water. This product water is
provided to the potable bus for the various users and may be stored in water bags for future use when the potable bus
needs supplementing. The WRS is comprised of the Urine Processor Assembly (UPA) and Water Processor Assembly
(WPA), which are located in two International Standard Payload Racks (ISPR) named WRS#1 and WRS#2. This
hardware was delivered to ISS on November 14, 2008, and is located in the ISS Node 3 module.

                II. Description of the ISS Water Recovery and Management System
    A conceptual schematic of the WRM is provided in Figure 1 and displays the inlets and outlets to both the WRS
and OGS since both systems are highly interconnected. Crew urine is collected in the Waste & Hygiene Compartment
(WHC) [1], which consists of a Russian Urinal system (referred to as the ACY). To maintain chemical and microbial
control of the urine and hardware, the urine is treated with an oxidizer and an inorganic acid. The pretreated urine is
delivered to the UPA to produce urine distillate and brine. In addition, pretreated urine collected in the Russian
Segment may also be manually transferred in Russian fluid containers (called ЕДВs) and transferred to the UPA. The
installation of the technology demonstration Brine Processor Assembly (BPA) has allowed further processing of the
urine brine, achieving >95% water recovered [2].




                 Figure 1. Water Recovery and Management Architecture for the ISS US Segment


    Condensate is collected from the cabin air by the Common Cabin Air Assembly (CCAA) Condensing Heat
Exchangers (CHXs). Urine distillate, humidity condensate and Sabatier product water are delivered to the WPA Waste
Tank for further processing. However, the Sabatier reactor has not been in service since October 2017 due to a failure
of the reactor, though replacement hardware is intended to be delivered to return this capability.
    Makeup water from Resupply Tanks (RSTs) launched from the ground is added to the WPA waste tank as required
to maintain the water balance. A separate Condensate Tank located in the US Laboratory Module is available as a
back-up in the event the WPA Waste Tank is unavailable for waste water collection.

     After the waste water is processed by the WRS, it is delivered to the potable bus. The potable bus is maintained at
a pressure of approximately 230 to 280 kPa (19 to 26.5 psig) so that water is available on demand for the various
users. Users of potable water from the bus include the Oxygen Generation Assembly (OGA), the WHC (for flush
water), the Potable Water Dispenser (PWD) and eXploration PWD (xPWD) for crew consumption, the Extravehicular
Mobility Unit (EMU) sublimator and Payloads. Finally, a reserve of a minimum of 818 L (1803 lbs) of potable water
is stored on ISS in Contingency Water Containers - Iodinated (CWC-Is), Water Storage System (WSS) Storage Tanks
(plumbed directly into the potable bus) and Water Resupply Tanks (RSTs) to maintain ISS operations in response to
contingency scenarios.
     Historically, the ISS Water Reserves threshold has been maintained at 818 liters to accommodate potable water to
crew, EVA, and oxygen generation during periods of water recovery outages. However, in early 2026, the ISS Program
has agreed to increase this reserve to 1413 liter in preparation for enhanced contingency vehicle cooling capabilities
arriving later this year. This system is called Supplemental Heat Rejection Evaporative Cooler (SHREC). This new
capability would require significant access to water to provide active cooling within the ISS Cabin. Therefore, to
reduce risk during an outage of the water management system while this contingency cooling capability is active, the
amount of reserved stored water must be increased.

A. Urine Processor Assembly
    A simplified schematic of the UPA is shown in Figure 3. Pretreated urine is delivered to the UPA either from the
US Segment WHC (outfitted with a Russian urinal) or via manual transfer from the Russian ЕДВ. In either case, the
composition of the pretreated urine is crew urine, flush water, and a pretreatment formula containing chromium
trioxide and an inorganic acid to inhibit microbial growth and the conversion of urea to ammonia. In the Russian
segment, the inorganic acid is sulfuric acid. In the US Segment, the inorganic acid has been switched to phosphoric
acid to address precipitation issues with calcium sulfate. The pretreated urine is pumped from the Waste Storage Tank
Assembly (WSTA) into the UPA recycle loop by the Fluids Control and Pump Assembly (FCPA). In the recycle loop,
the pretreated urine is recirculated through the Distillation Assembly (DA), the Advanced Recycle Filter Tank
Assembly (ARFTA), a brine filter, and back to the DA. Distillate produced in the DA is pumped to the WPA Waste
Water Tank. The DA consists of a rotating centrifuge where the waste urine stream is evaporated at low pressure. The
                                                            3
                                  International Conference on Environmental Systems
vapor is compressed and condensed on the opposite side of the evaporator surface to conserve latent energy. A rotary
lobe compressor provides the driving force for the evaporation and compression of water vapor. Waste brine resulting
from the distillation process is stored in the ARFTA, which is a bellows tank that can be filled and drained on ISS.
When the brine is concentrated to the required limit, the ARFTA is emptied into an ЕДВ or offloaded into the BPA.
The urine brine for disposal is delivered into available expendable containers (ex: empty Resupply Tanks, or expiring
ЕДВs) or into the Russian Rodnik tank on the Progress vehicle for disposal. The ARFTA is refilled with pretreated
urine to initiate a new concentration cycle. The Pressure Control and Pump Assembly (PCPA) is four-tube peristaltic
purge pump which provides for the removal of non-condensable gases and water vapor from the DA. Liquid cooling
of the pump housing promotes condensation, thus reducing the required volumetric capacity of the peristaltic pump.
Gases and condensed water are pumped to the Separator Plumbing Assembly (SPA), which recovers and returns water
from the purge gases to the product distillate stream. A Firmware Controller Assembly (FCA) provides the command
control, excitation, monitoring, and data downlink for UPA sensors and effectors.




                                 Figure 3. Urine Processor Assembly Schematic

    The UPA was designed to process a nominal load of 9 kg/day (19.8 lbs/day) of wastewater consisting of urine and
flush water. This is the expected quantity for a 6-crew load on ISS. The UPA was designed to recover 85% of the
water content from the pretreated urine, though issues with urine quality encountered in 2009 required the recovery to
be dropped to 75% for the US Segment and 70% for urine collected in the Russian Segment. Implementation of a
phosphate-based urine pretreatment [7] in early 2016 allowed the UPA to return to a minimum of 85% recovery of
urine collected in the US Segment, though urine recovery from urine collected in the Russian Segment remains at 70%
because no changes have been made to the pretreatment in the Russian Segment. Continued assessments of returned
brine filter samples have allowed incremental increases of water recovery from US pretreated urine from 85% to 87%.
Targeted final percent recovery from US pretreated urine in the UPA is planned for 90%; however, further analysis
of returned samples is required.

B. Water Processor Assembly Overview
    An updated simplified schematic of the WPA is provided in Figure 4. The WPA feed water includes humidity
condensate, distillate from the UPA, and Sabatier product water when available. Wastewater first passes through a
300 micron External Filter Assembly (EFA) to capture any biofilm from the Waste Water Tank (WWT). The water is
initially degassed by the Mostly Liquid Separator (MLS), and then pumped through a 0.5 micron particulate filter
followed by a Multifiltration (MF) Bed containing adsorbent and ion exchange media. Prior to 2019, the WPA used

                                                         4
                                International Conference on Environmental Systems
two MF beds in series but switched to a single MF bed in July 2019. Volatile organics not effectively removed by the
MF bed are oxidized in the Catalytic Reactor at elevated temperature, which also removes microbial contamination.
Excess oxygen and gaseous oxidation by-products are removed by the Gas Liquid Separator (GLS). Dissolved and
soluble reaction byproducts are removed by the Ion Exchange Bed, which also adds iodine to the product water as a
biocide. Product water is stored in the Water Storage Tank prior to delivery to the ISS potable water bus.

                             IV. Water Recovery and Management Status
    Between February of 2024 and February 2026, WPA has produced approximately 17,900 L (39,000 lbs) of product
water. In addition, 2,558 L (5,640 lbs) of total ISS potable water is currently stored on ISS for resupply water and in
reserve to protect for contingencies. Management of the water mass balance has improved with the delivery of the
Water Storage System (WSS) in the US Segment. This surplus of water in the US Segment is expected to shift with
the installation of a replacement distiller for the Russian Segment Urine Processor and a most recent delivery of an
additional urine processor in the Multipurpose Laboratory Module (MLM). Installation of the first Urine Processor
was completed in 2018, but the distiller failed during initial checkout. Recovery of the system was achieved; however,
only for a limited time. Hardware failures and system vacuum leaks have been ongoing challenges for both systems
and only recently one of the distillers has shown progress in successful urine processing. Distillate from the Russian
Urine Processors are planned to be used for flush water, feed water to the Russian Elektron, and feed water to their
condensate processor pending successful results from analysis of distillate samples returned to the ground.

    1. Further Water Management Expansions
    Several additional modifications to the ISS water system have been delivered and installed, including a Urine
Transfer System (UTS), a Universal Waste Management System (UWMS), and a Brine Processor Assembly (BPA).
The BPA increases the total water recovery of water from urine brine generated by the UPA to beyond 95%. Thus
when combined with the efficiencies of the Water Processor Assembly, the total water recovery aboard the ISS is
98+% when BPA is in operation. The UWMS, also known as “Toilet,” for ISS applications, is a US-designed toilet.
With increased crew sizes on the ISS, providing additional waste management has been a critical path to ensure crew
comfort as well as management the current Waste and Hygiene Compartment (WHC). These systems play a critical
role to understand advanced technologies necessary for next generation exploration missions. The UWMS is a new
toilet designed by Collins Aerospace. This hardware is planned for use in the Orion vehicle, and full demonstration of
the hardware on ISS is being pursued.

   Brine Processor Assembly (BPA)

    The BPA has been installed on the ISS since March 2021. The BPA has been operating on ISS as a Technology
Demonstration for NASA Exploration missions for the first three (3) years of installation. BPA has processed brine
generated by the UPA to further remove water to add to that recovered by the Distillation Assembly and thereby
achieve ~98% water recovery. The system utilizes a specially designed membrane-based bladder that allows water
vapor to pass through (via membrane distillation) with the aid of heated forced convection. The final water reclamation
is achieved by established humidity control systems aboard the ISS. The system was delivered to NASA by Paragon
Space Development and has since completed 81 dewatering cycles since first activation in early 2021. Since February
2024, the BPA has completed 43 dewatering cycles, yielding approximately 946 liters of urine brine. Of that, it is
estimated ~780 liters of water have been returned back to humidity condensate, a significant cost saving in potential
resupply mass. In 2024, BPA also processed 1 bladder of pretreated urine (PTU) providing additional data for future
potential BPA-like hardware to perform urine processing. Teams also got another opportunity to process another batch
of PTU via BPA, leveraging nominal ARFTA flush operations that would have otherwise disposed of this volume. In
anticipation of these operations, ground testing at the NASA Marshall Space Flight Center (MSFC) validated
successful operations and further insights for bladder reuse configurations [10]. Processing PTU via BPA would allow
an alternate option for processing urine directly if the UPA has an extended downtime period. Moreover, the BPA has
provided a significant reduction in trash management and logistics by reducing the total volume of brine for disposal
(trashing off the ISS vehicle). Further details on BPA operation and accomplishments can be found in separate
conference papers [2,12]. A successful demonstration of this technology is considered a critical step prior to future
manned missions beyond ISS (i.e., a mission to Mars) because of the necessity to recover as much water as reasonably
possible due to the launch costs for water and the absence of resupply capability. With continued success, NASA will
continue to use the technology on ISS to reduce the water resupply requirement from earth.
    In 2025 the BPA received new outlet exhaust hardware: 1) A new, dedicated ducting 2) A new exhaust outlet,
addressing sealing concerns from a previously 3D printed component, and 3) new humidity and flow velocity sensors
integrated into the new exhaust ducting. An initial activation of the sensors confirmed viability in giving ground
engineering teams direct insights to the humid air exiting a BPA throughout a dewatering cycle. The timing was such
that the teams could assess baseline brine dewatering with the PTU run to capture conservative high and conservative
low water impartment loads into the cabin.
    Some unexpected anomalies were observed in 2024 and 2025 in the thermal responses associated with a specific
inlet temperature switch located on the BPA heater assembly. Despite these anomalies, the most significant milestone
was achieved in terms of overall water recovery and the progress of the technology demonstration. Refer to
publications [13, 14] for more details.

   eXploration Potable Water Dispenser (xPWD)

     For exploration missions beyond Low Earth Orbit, there are expectations that these missions will enter periods of
uncrewed, dormant phases. These exploration missions will also be challenged with minimizing upmass and
consumables. The Potable Water Dispenser (PWD) is the point at which crew receives potable water for food, water,
and even payload operations. For crew consumption purposes, there needs to be assurance that quality water is
maintained. For the PWD operations, the use of a microbial filter at point of use is required to prevent the passage of
microbes to the crew. Moreover, the use of the activated carbon and ion exchange cartridge (ACTEX) is deployed to
filter out the total iodine (biocide of choice for US water recycling) for crew health concerns. The baseline designs of
the PWD had some early challenges due to identified dead-legs within the internal water loops and bypasses that
caused microbial disruptions during initial stowage duration. To that end, in the pursuit of exploration mission
objectives and enhancing dormancy success on PWD while also upgrading capabilities and functionalities, an
eXploration Potable Water Dispenser (xPWD) was funded and activated on the ISS September 2023. The full status
of the ISS xPWD can be found in a concurrent publication [15].

2. Water Transfers and ACTEX Throughput
    Over the last several years there has been an increase in requests for US water transfers to the Russians due to
prioritization of manifest hardware and agreed-to Protocols between the US and RS. This has led the USOS to
routinely deliver potable water to RS EDVs. For the foreseeable future, this support is expected to continue. Because
RS ECLSS hardware requires de-iodinated water, each USOS-to-RS potable water transfer must include Activated
Carbon/Ion Exchange (ACTEX) treatment to remove iodine/iodide for RS compatibility. Historically, there have been
two primary delivery methods to fill EDVs from the USOS. The first is to launch RSTs prefilled with iodinated potable
water, then transfer via a fluid transfer pump through an ACTEX; however, if no RSTs are available alternate means
have been utilized. The second method is to supply from the Water Storage System (WSS) potable tanks, which, by
established WSS operations to protect against stagnate potable water in Shuttle style tanks with susceptibility to
leachates, requires the use of an internal ACTEX cartridge + MCV resign that re-iodinates the potable water.
Therefore, for this particular water transfer mode simultaneously adds throughput through two ACTEX unnecessarily
(one on the WSS outlet and one at point of use for the offload) and therefore imposes a heavy burden on ACTEX
spares and throughput margins. The currently installed and utilized ACTEXs are with a configuration that uses
Ambersorb sorbent. It has a wetted service life of four years or a throughput limit of approximately 6,750 lb (3,062
L) of water. Increased RS water transfer demand has accelerated ACTEX usage, necessitating more frequent Remove
and Replace (R&R) actions to remain within these limits. Given finite logistics and the importance of maintaining de-
iodination capability for both WHC and RS support, reducing unnecessary ACTEX throughput is a priority. To address
these constraints, water transfers to the RS with de-iodinated water from the PWD method was implemented to support
RS EDV fills using WPA product water with a single external ACTEX. This approach bypasses the WSS potable tank
pathway and eliminates the need to run two ACTEX cartridges in parallel. By relying on one external ACTEX to de-
iodinate WPA product water before transfer, the method significantly reduces overall ACTEX throughput demand
while meeting RS water quality requirements. The rationale for adopting this method is threefold. First, it leverages
an established procedure, offering operational simplicity and straightforward execution. Second, it minimizes RS crew
training burden, which is expected to be limited and manageable. Third, it avoids wasteful simultaneous exhaustion
of two ACTEX cartridges inherent in WSS method, preserving scarce On-Orbit spares. This conservation is critical:
if ACTEX logistic is not able to meet such high throughput demand, the USOS would be unable to provide de-
iodinated water to the RSOS. Consequently, curbing unnecessary usage, particularly on the WSS outlet ACTEX, is
warranted.
    This PWD configuration, although is convenient from the items above, a third option is actively being looked into
to re-assess the established WSS procedures for use of its internal ACTEX + MCV cartridges as the potable water in
these tanks are being cycled much more frequently than originally designed; therefore, risk of leachates are
significantly reduced. Moreover, as the success of the xPWD continues, there could be Programmatic decision points
to allow the legacy PWD to retire, thus omitting access to a by-pass port for these particular water transfer activities.

2. FrankenPump
    As alluded above, end of 2023 into 2024 was a challenging period for PCPA performance. In November 2023, the
first 2nd generation planetary drive PCPA was installed. This ORU experienced a premature failure of the pump
motor drive train. In Feb 2024, this PCPA was replaced with an older, legacy design harmonic drive PCPA, with
expected limited remaining operational life for peristaltic tube install. This PCPA operated for 4 additional months
before failing due to ruptured peristaltic tubing. At this point, on orbit, there was a PCPA with failed motor, viable
pump ‘body’, and a failed ‘body’ but working motor. Ground teams devised a plan to perform Intermediate-level (I-
level) maintenance to take components from the two (2) non-functioning ORUs. A working motor was removed from
the PCPA with the failed peristaltic tubing and was installed into the PCPA with a failed motor. This level of
maintenance was never designed to be performed on ISS in microgravity but was performed flawlessly by
crewmembers. This newly created PCPA was named “FrankenPump”. The circumstances, challenges and recoveries
behind these events are further explored in a concurrent publication [17]. This FrankenPump PCPA operated for a
couple of weeks and provided enough time for a pristine spare to arrive on ISS, installed after FrankenPump
experienced a motor failure. The new, pristine spare PCPA is a 1 st generation planetary drive. This ORU (the fifth
since November 2023) is still installed and performing nominally with a current runtime of 2,381 hours, 99% of the
predicted operational life.

3. Urine Back-Log Management
    The Purge Pump issues and failures seen this year during UPA operations created multiple down times with the
UPA in a non-functional state. During these down times, extra PTU was collected that could not be processed. In order
to not throw away potential good potable water, this PTU was stowed in containers and saved to be processed when
UPA was back operating nominally. Approximately 300 L of PTU was collected during the various times UPA was
not operating. This stored PTU required a Flight Rule waiver so that it’s storage time would not expire while waiting
to be processed. As PTU ages, the formation of solids increases and pH increases (while still remaining in the acidic
range) can also occur within the storage containers. The waiver allowed additional time for the PTU to remain in a
stowage container and stipulated that before being processed it will be filtered through the urine filter hose nominally
used only for Russian Segment PTU. Historically, when the UPA has processed bad PTU with excessive biological
growth, there has been degraded performance and failures within the system. Once the UPA was fully operational
again, all of the backlog of PTU was successfully processed and no PTU had to be disposed. Roughly 180 L of PTU
exceeded the original expiration date and required the additional time allowed by the Flight Rule waiver on expiration
time. UPA data reviews were able to determine that this PTU processed through the UPA nominally. Having this
additional data regarding aged PTU provides additional insight to compare with the under-treated PTU problems
experienced last year that led to issues with UPA processing ability.

                              VI. Water Processor Assembly Current Status
    The WPA has processed increasing amounts of wastewater as average crew sizes continue to increase on the ISS.
Despite the increased throughput, the WPA continues to operate well. Since February of 2024, the WPA saw several
major ORU replacements where all but one were replaced due to expected end of life performance. Since last reporting,
the WPA required replacement of the Wastewater Filter (informally known as the EFA), Particulate Filter, two
catalytic reactors, and the Ion Exchange (IX) Bed ORU. In addition, since last reporting the installed MF Bed has
since reached the first ionic breakthrough at 8,000 lbs. Teams will continue to monitor for the second and third ionic
breakthrough, estimated around 12,500 lbs and 27,000 lbs respectively, but the intent is to keep this MF bed installed
until such breakthrough occurs even if throughput surpasses 27,000 lbs.

1. Particulate Filter ORU
    The overall size of this filter allows for extended installed operation within an ISS architecture. The baselined
design life was listed at 3 years of operation before requiring a replacement ORU. There have only been two
replacements of this filter: one in 2013 after 4 years of operation, and the second this year after an incredible 4251
days (11.6 years). For some background, the first particulate filter was replaced in 2013 after available telemetry
suggested this filter to be the cause of some highly variable differential pressure (dP) readings across the filter. When
the filter was replaced with a pristine filter, the variability slightly improved but did not resolve. It was later determined
that a faulty check valve (CV) upstream of the filter was the primary culprit. After removal of this CV, the high
variability dP response fully resolved, thus indicating the original particulate filter did not need to be removed in 2013.

2. Catalytic Reactor ORUs
    Over the last two years since previous reporting, the WPA saw multiple catalytic reactor replacements. The first
was for Catalytic Reactor SN007, a legacy design with elastomeric seals. This failure was a result of expected end-of-
life external water leakage, common for a legacy design catalytic reactor. All legacy design catalytic reactors exhibit
a degree of external leakage isolated to the end cap seals of the catalyst cylinders, causing the WPA to fault due to
water accumulation on critical electronics. At the time of the SN007 failure (May 2024), the Upgraded Catalytic
Reactor SN001 was on board and ready to be installed. Also onboard were two legacy cat reactors, (legacy) SN001
and SN006, but they were not the ideal replacement units, as there was a desire among the ISS engineering teams to
install the upgraded unit and obtain valuable runtime data for exploration assessments.
    Although Program and Project agreement was in place to install the Upgraded Catalytic Reactor SN001,
unfortunately, Catalytic Reactor SN006 was installed by mistake. Furthermore, the unit was already powered up, had
reached required system temperatures, and was in the process of completing a flush with wastewater through the unit
before teams realized this installation error. Engineering requested that the Upgraded Catalytic Reactor SN001 be
installed immediately so that the engineering teams would not need to wait for ~2 years for SN006 to fail, and would
not have to limit the Tech Demo period and assessment of the upgraded refurbishment processes (which have not yet
demonstrated an ability to prevent a recurrence of a previous failure of the Demo Catalytic Reactor).
    It was agreed to do the swap of SN006 to the Upgraded Catalytic Reactor SN001. Before that could be completed,
preparation of the SN006 was required as unsterilized wastewater had entered the unit and there was a risk that
biofouling could have occurred while in storage. To best prepare the catalytic reactor for stowage and against the
effects of exposure to unsterilized wastewater, the unit had been allowed to reach its thermal stabilization temperature
to achieve sterility and allowed to cool before its removal from the ISS rack. This was a procedure developed from
prior experience: it had been established that thermal cycles and time at high temperature contribute significantly to
the degradation of elastomeric seals that led to the legacy catalytic reactor failure modes. This procedure may have
adversely affected its time of expected end of life but the magnitude of the impact, if any, the limited accidental
installed time has done to the SN006 lifespan is unclear.
    The Upgraded Catalytic Reactor SN001 was finally installed and successfully activated. There were some expected
operational misbehaviors due to a known and accepted (prior to launch) failed thermocouple but overall, the unit was
holding pressure and temperature and was generating appropriate quality water. Unfortunately, approximately two
weeks into operations, the catalytic reactor exhibited severe external water leakage. Telemetry suggested the water
leakage was located within the catalytic reactor cylinders; thus it is assumed that this was yet another recurrence to

the previous development test objective (DTO) leakage with the new metal seal upgrades. To recover WPA operation,
the unit was removed and replaced with the already wetted SN006 and the system was able to recover back to nominal
operations. The failed upgraded catalytic reactor has since been returned to the ground testing teams and is awaiting
ground investigation to determine cause of failure.
    The SN006 catalytic reactor operated for 419 days and processed over 23,000 lbs of water, showing intermittent
loss of pressures throughout the install, failing once sustained loss of pressure due to external water leakage occurred.
The (legacy) Catalytic Reactor SN001 was then installed in July 2025, showing occasional decay in pressure
management but not impacting nominal operations. These intermittent decay in pressure profiles that do not fault
WPA are suspected to be internal water leakage through downstream regulators and not due to external water leakage.

3. Ion Exchange Bed ORU
    As discussed in a previous report, the removed and replaced Ion Exchange (IX) Bed had become the fleet leader
for the amount of water processed while active in WPA. As a reminder, this bed is the final scrubber for the WPA that
removes the remaining byproducts from the catalytic reactor and imparts iodine to the clean water. The IX bed has
been sized such that the predicted end of life signature can be monitored in real time from a downstream conductivity
sensor, the data from which can indicate ionic breakthrough. However, the amount and levels of the iodine cannot be
monitored in real time and requires taking water samples for offline monitoring. The increased crew size and the
activation of the Brine Processor Assembly have added more available water to be reclaimed and recycled through
the WPA in an accelerated rate compared to historical WPA production rates. The amount of water processed in the
IX bed has correspondingly increased but remains within the originally planned preventative maintenance of three
years of service life. Therefore, engineering teams requested additional iodine samples to increase the data resolution
of measured iodine depletion in order to update the estimated projected life in a way that can reflect the increased
throughput. The updated estimates provided the much-needed confidence to allow the IX bed to continue operation
past the average throughput limit. In May 2024, the IX bed was preemptively removed and replaced before seeing an
ionic breakthrough, but after the IX bed reached just over 50,000 lbs of throughput.

                                    VII. Conclusion and Future Work
     Since the 2024 reporting period, the WRS has continued to provide the ISS crew with potable water, the Oxygen
Generation System with water to electrolyze, the Waste & Hygiene Compartment with flush water, water for crew
hygiene, and water for use on payloads. During this time, the WPA required the MF bed to be replaced after reaching
its anticipated end of life signature and has seen a new milestone in IX bed operation with processing the most water
in a given installation with no issues in imparting proper iodine levels. With the use of the legacy catalytic reactors,
it was expected that every 1.5+ years the external water leakage through the elastomeric seals would recurred. To that
end, over the course of this current reporting periods, two catalytic reactors were replaced to due the legacy failure
mode and one additional failure, prematurely, on the Upgraded Catalytic Reactor – likely stemming from continued
metal seal design and integration challenges. The root cause will be unclear until the unit enters its ground
investigation. Until then, the next line of catalytic reactor spares will selectively target other available upgrades to
strike a balance between exploration checkouts on orbit and critical sparing posture risks.
     The UPA has seen historical operational runtime hours on the Distillation Assembly and Fluids Control and Pump
Assembly, reaching well over 300% of predicted runtime each. This is a testament to years of dedicated upgrades
work that addressed a series of challenges within each ORU. In contrast, the UPA has also seen several purge pump
failures from a variety of failure causes [16]. Despite the higher number for purge pump ORU R&Rs, only one
premature failure mode was exhibited on “Criticality 1” level hardware. The UPA continues to see increases in
operational runtime hours overall due to increased crew sizes and continued processing of Russian urine. Despite this
increased duty cycle, the UPA has continued operating with no apparent issues related to throughput increase.
     The increase in water transfers from the US to the RS found increased throughput of the on-orbit ACTEXs driving
changes in planned activities, transitioning RS water transfers to the PWD-based procedure with a single external
ACTEX. This maintains de-iodination performance while materially reducing ACTEX throughput and sparing
consumption. This strategy sustains ongoing cross-segment water support, aligns with hardware life constraints, and
strengthens logistics resilience for continued RS transfer operations.
     The ELCSS communities continue to look to future exploration missions. Critical paths of challenging the next
generation ELCSS technologies in the relevant space environment are finally being realized. The upgraded MF beds,
BPA, UTS, and UPA’s DA and planetary gears (as found in the fluids and purge pumps) have all been integrated on
the ISS and are showing exceptional performance. The xPWD ISS operation and runtime with enhanced UV light
