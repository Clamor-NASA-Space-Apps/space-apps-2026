                      The Brine Processor Assembly (BPA) Ground Testbed Unit located at Marshall Space
                  Flight Center (MSFC) allows test to support ISS and future Exploration architectures. The
                  BPA ground unit is flight-like which allows for direct testing and assessments for ISS nominal
                  and contingency operational planning and troubleshooting insights. This unit has also received
                  upgraded design solutions for improved insights and performance. Moreover, focus on future
                  exploration missions beyond Low Earth Orbit are in mind to establish thorough
                  understanding of reliability and maintainability. A series of ground tests performed at MSFC
                  included the use and reuse of a new, pristine bladder filled up to 22.5 L pretreated, augmented
                  urine (PTAU) to perform a dewatering cycle and subsequent cycles reusing the same bladder.
                  These dewatering cycles were assessed primarily for dewatering production rates and residual
                  brine tracking from the result of reusing the bladder. A total of three (3) dewatering cycles
                  performed reusing a bladder. The total water recovery for each dewatering run decreased
                  with each new dewatering run, from 95.4% down to 80.0% recovery, and initial processing
                  rates decreased from 6.0 lbs/day (2.7 kg/day) to 3.5 lbs/day (1.6 kg/day). Overall, the results
                  confirm BPA’s flexibility in processing pretreated urine directly; however, reuse of the
                  bladder, although is viable, may not trade well for required wastewater recover rate needs.
                  During testing, unplanned interruptions and notable observations occurred. These will be
                  discussed in further detail. Preliminary testing has also looked to understand potential
                  removal and flush operations of residual brine between dewatering to improve production
                  and water recovery to better improve consumable utilization. The BPA Ground Testbed has
                  provided excellent performance insights for potential ISS contingency planning as well as
                  initial assessments for Exploration reliability insights. Future testing will look to further
                  understand ISS BPA performances while continuing reliability assessments.

                                      I. Introduction and Background

T    HE Brine Processor Assembly (BPA) was launched to the International Space Station (ISS) on February 2021
     and installed on March 2021 to expand and increase the total water recovery from urine brine generated by the
Urine Processor Assembly (UPA) to beyond 95% [1]. BPA utilizes the forced convection of spacecraft cabin air
coupled with a robust membrane distillation process to recover purified water from 22.5 liters of brine [2] [3]. The
BPA operates on the ISS as a Technology Demonstration for NASA Exploration missions and processes the brine
generated by the UPA to remove water and thereby achieving ~98% water recovery [1]. A BPA Ground Test Bed
with flight-like components was delivered and installed at NASA Marshall Space Flight Center (MSFC) and initial
system checks started in 2023. This ground testbed provides the capability of performing experimental and
troubleshooting efforts on-ground to support any on-orbit operations.

B. Ground Test Bed Goals
    The BPA Ground Test Bed objectives will focus on three (3) critical areas. One will establish flight-like relevant
operations to aid in troubleshooting or further understanding of on-orbit operations. Another is strictly reliability
metric based focusing on operational runtime to explore failure modes and enhance statistical understanding for
exploration sparing/mass trade studies. And finally, this testbed will allow for upgraded design integration to improve
identified performance and/or reliability enhancements.
    The BPA Ground Test Bed also provides better insights to operations associated with water recovery efforts thanks
to the flexibility of the ground test bed infrastructure and test executions. It allows for simulation of dewatering cycles
utilizing brine and/or PTAU to support ISS troubleshooting needs. The BPA Ground Test Bed offers the ability to
provide insights for potential ISS contingency planning and perform reliability assessments to the system. Ultimately,
it will be used first to establish baseline operations and understanding within NASA’s engineering and Programs to
then allow for critical technology advancements as exploration architectures are more realized.

C. BPA Test Bed Operations
    The BPA is designed such that pretreated urine brine can be processed and recover water that otherwise would
have been trashed during crewed missions. The ISS UPA is excellent at recovery water from urine; however, this
technology is unable to recover to that near 100% due to the recycling nature of urine brine through the system. The
BPA utilized forced convection of heated cabin air to enhance membrane distillation rates across an advanced bladder
technology [3] [4].
    To generate Pretreated Urine (PTU) for ground operations, the BPA test bed Team at Marshall Space Flight Center
(MSFC) collected urine from volunteer donors. This urine was then pretreated using the Alternate (“Alt”) Phosphoric
based pretreatment recipe. In addition to the pretreatment, the urine was augmented to reflect additional non-water
solids and potential precipitates to simulate the high concentration of contaminants found in crew urine at the ISS.
This provides the potential to evaluate the effects of low mass fraction brine vs high mass fraction brine (ex: Pretreated
Augmented Urine 50th Percentile (PTAU50) vs Pretreated Augmented Urine 95th Percentile (PTAU95)) and/or to
introduce and mix dewatering cycles with brine and PTAU. The PTAU95 will reflect the “worst-case” scenario with
an increased solid fraction making the system performance approx. 10-30% worse in terms of processing rate and total
processed urine than it would be with nominal astronaut Pretreated Urine (PTU) [5].

                                    III. Operations and Test Campaign

A. Initial System Checks
    Prior to the introduction of any wastewater processing (ex: urine brine or PTAU) into the system, a sequence of
deionized water test runs was performed with the intent to qualify and troubleshoot internal controls software as well
as prove full functionality of the brine transfer lines, heaters, fan, and data recording systems. Teams also became
familiar with bladder install and fill procedures, and post-processing inspections. A total of four (4) deionized water
test runs performed in the system, at targeted heater set points, to help identify and troubleshoot issues. Successful
checkouts and expected performances were completed, confirming the BPA Ground Test Bed as fully operational for
next phase of testing.

B. Pretreated Augmented Urine (PTAU) Test Series
    During 2023-2024, the ISS UPA experiences several instances of recovery phases, which limited the rate at which
urine was being actively processed [6]. There was a desire to investigate the ability of the ISS BPA as a means to
process the pretreated urine rather than the UPA generated brine as a way to get ahead in urine back-log, or possibly
a stop gap ‘urine processor.’ With access to the ground BPA unit, opportunity to validate the operational expectation
of the ISS BPA was explored. Moreover, the additional potential flexibility of reusing a bladder for PTU processing
was also discussed due to the expected lower reduced mass volume allowing for limited ullage for refills.
    To support the ISS in future, contingency troubleshooting or stop-gap urine processing efforts, the ground testbed
team utilized a new pristine bladder filled with Pretreated Augmented Urine 50th Augmented (PTAU50) to perform
a dewatering cycle and subsequent cycles reusing the same bladder. Test objectives looked to understand production
rates with each dewatering cycle, observations of the bladder itself for pore wetting, and tracking the total fill volume
to ensure no overfilling would occur. A total of three (3) dewatering cycles of PTAU performed reusing one bladder.

for max water recovery was noted to occur just over Day 10. The additional 2 days were allowed to ensure capture of
final baseline conditions. The final deltaT of this cycle was 4.9°C.

2. Test Cycle 2:
    A second cycle was completed reusing the same installed bladder containing approximately 2.3 lbs (1.04 kg) of
residual brine inside the bladder. The bladder design can accommodate over 30% more capacity (conservative) to a
nominal working fill volume; therefore, this remaining 2.3 lbs (1.04 kg) of residual brine was determined would not
impact another refill of 50.0 lbs (22.7 kg) to max fill capacity for a bladder. The BPA testbed mass at the beginning

a total of five (5) interruptions. Most of these interruptions remained for less than 24 hrs. and included software
interruptions, facility power outages and planned room/facility maintenance. One (1) major interruption continued for
approximately two (2) weeks due to facilities testbed integration and software/controls upgrades. During this stagnant
period the bladder experienced “pore wetting” and odor was permeating from the bladder. Test was resumed and the
cycle was finalized with 42.7 lbs (19.4 kg) of water removed (81.6% REC by mass) with a starting average processing
rate of 4 lbs/day (1.8 kg/day) but significantly reduced after each interruption. The final BPA testbed mass after the
dewatering cycle was 123.3 lbs (55.9 kg) as shown in Figure 4. An apparent mass gain is observed during the two (2)
week stagnant period; however, these are fluctuations of the BPA scale while the team was opening the BPA lid for
bladder inspection and documentation of the pore wetting progression.

3. Test Cycle 3:
    A third cycle was completed by reusing the same bladder containing approximately 4.31 L of cumulative brine.
The MSFC Ground Testbed Team decided to challenge the bladder a third time understanding potential reduction in
the processing and recovery rates as well as the pore wetting already experienced in this bladder. A safety assessment
was performed and determined to continue with filling operations and monitor closely both the refilling process and
the dewatering cycle and terminate at any point to safely dispose of the bladder. The BPA testbed mass at the beginning
of the refilling process was 123.5 lbs (56.0 kg). The bladder was refilled with an additional 50.3 lbs (22.8 kg) of PTAU
for a total combined BPA testbed mass of 173.8 lbs (78.8 kg). This last cycle had no interruptions and was concluded

difference between the system temperatures which is expected at the end of a cycle. The final BPA testbed mass after
the dewatering cycle was 126.0 lbs (57.2 kg). The cycle was finalized with a new total of 12.1 lbs (5.5 kg) of combined
residual brine. This third cycle had no interruptions as shown in Figure 5, but due to the slow processing rate the entire
dewatering cycle continued for approximately 25 days total in comparison with Test Cycle 1 with a 12-day duration.

4. Other Observations
    Between each dewatering cycle the team attempted to obtain samples from the residual brine but no significant
volume was available and the high viscosity of the residual brine made it difficult to accomplish. The estimated total
downtime between each PTAU test cycle took on average 7-8 days providing enough time to clean and sanitize the
BPA fill tank and refill operations. Figure 6 shows the bladder top surface section during the entire PTAU test
campaign. Pore wetting can be observed at the end of the PTAU Test Cycle 2. This occurs due to pore fouling, rather
than pressure-induced rupture of the inner bladder [7]; however, once a pore is fouled, it is expected that induced
pressures may encourage liquid transport at a faster rate. The inner bladder relies on hydrophobic properties to prevent
liquid water from crossing over; however, these pores (shape, surface properties, etc) can be susceptible to deposition
of foulants degrading that hydrophobicity. The fouling, in these cases, occurs primarily due to the inner bladder being
exposed to the pretreated urine over-time. Depending on the quality of urine, the variability of urine constituents, and
duration of exposure can influence the severity of pore wetting. To that end, the team believes that 1) the use and reuse
of the bladder and 2) the downtime experienced during the dewatering cycle allowed opportunity for the pores to foul.
Similar pore wetting features were observed with returned-to-ground ISS BPA bladders for ground analysis. On-orbit,
there were very little pore wetting features when packed for returned, but once the bladder arrived on the ground and
travel time. It is likely the time duration and returned flight loads enhanced the pore wettability of the internal
membrane [7].




Figure 6. Bladder inside the BPA Ground Test Bed System at various PTAU Test Cycles. Pore wetting
observed on Test Cycles 2 & 3.
     Around this same time of the PTAU Test Cycle 2, the BPA unit, triggered a brine leak alarm on the BPA system.
Upon inspection of the leak sensor and internals of the BPA system, no evidence of a PTAU leak was found. The BPA
is equipped with a custom brine leak sensor located on the outlet end of the BPA Detrusor to provide direct monitoring
for any external leakage of brine from the bladder. The fan draws air across the bladder and this sensor is designed
such that this nature convection and motive force of mass through the unit, via this fan, will allow for brine leak
detection. During ground testing, a green-brown discoloration was observed around the leak sensor indicating potential
corrosion of the active leak detection circuit design. A similar event was experienced on-orbit in August 2023 when
the leak sensor was triggered at the ISS. In that case it was presumed that the corrosion caused an increase in
conductivity causing a false-positive alarm trip. A successful cleaning operation by the crew at the ISS was performed
as a mitigation strategy and the life of the leak sensor was extended [8]. The MSFC BPA Testbed team attempted
cleaning operations, without success, and ultimately decided to disconnect the leak sensor and continue operations to
finalize the test campaign. At the end of the Test Cycle 3 additional pore wetting was observed in the bladder but no
external PTAU leaks were found. Bladder was properly disposed and the BPA system was inspected and cleaned. A
summary of the PTAU test campaign including odor descriptions is provided in Table 1. By the end of the Test Cycle
3 it was evident that a strong odor was permeating from the bladder and the BPA system. The qualitative odor analysis
was performed by the MSCF Team with the bladder located inside the BPA system (detrusor and BPA lid closed).

C. Pre-Treated-Augmented Urine (PTAU) & Brine Test Series
    The next test series consisted of a total of three (3) cycles utilizing PTAU50 for the first cycle, UPA generated
brine at the ISS like 87% Recovery (REC) for the second cycle and a mix of 50% PTAU50 and 50% PTAU95. This
test series represents a strategic test matrix to understand the influences of water recovery on-orbit if an ISS BPA
processes PTU but then refilled with variable urine/brine quality. The previous test confirmed refilling is possible with
some observed decline in production rate but still viable. However, the impacts of refilling the bladder with brine that
has even higher solids fractions has not been explored for a reused bladder. Furthermore, teams want to explore if any
influences from a variable pretreated urine with increased organic and inorganic loads would occur. As before, the
bladder was reused with the residual cumulative brine content remaining inside the bladder. One additional goal of
this second test series was to minimize the total interruptions during cycles and the total downtime between each
dewatering cycle to reduce any potential of pore wetting as experienced in the PTAU test cycles.
1. Test Cycle 1:
    A new bladder was installed in the system and PTAU50 was used to fill the bladder with an initial volume of 22.5
L. The total BPA system mass at the beginning of the cycle was 163.8 lbs (74.3 kg) and the final mass of the system
at the of the cycle was 116.8 lbs (53.0 kg). This first cycle provided an average of 5 lbs/day (2.3 kg/day) processing
rate in the beginning of the dewatering cycle with a total cycle duration of approximately 10 days to reach max water
recovery efficiencies. This test had no interruptions or major incidents across those 10 operational days.
Approximately 3.2 lbs (1.5 kg) of residual brine was left inside the bladder after the 10 day cycle with a total of 47.0
lbs (21.3 kg) processed (93.4% REC by mass). These results were in-family to the initial PTU Test Series.

  D. Flush and Cleaning Test
      The intent of this test was to study and understand the efficiencies or gains from applying a cleaning flush
  procedure in between dewatering cycles and determine an effective cleaning method post-dewatering operations.
  Previous test results from PTAU dewatering cycles confirmed dewatering rates decreased by approx. 58% while
  reusing bladders. The goal was to reduce the overall organic minerals contents by >75% and reuse the bladder with
  the same fluid type (UPA-brine 87% REC). There are limited number of BPA flight-like bladders available on-ground
  at a given time thus confirming viability to reuse these bladders with an effective cleaning procedure can offer
  significant cost savings and the opportunity to expand the bladders lifespan.
      A new bladder was installed in the BPA ground testbed system to process UPA-brine 87%. The first cycle started
  with a total of 22.7 L of UPA-brine transferred into the bladder and ended with approximately 17.6 lbs (8.0 kg) of
  residual brine at the end of the 14-day cycle (processed about 34.1 lbs (15.5 kg) for 66% REC by mass). Initial
  processing rate of approximately 5 lbs/day (2.3 kg/day) occurred in the first days of the cycle. After 10 days the
                                                          9

converging, achieving a delta Temp of <6°C. This helps as an indication of potential conclusion of the dewatering

                                     IV. Conclusions and Forward Work
    The BPA Ground Test Bed at MSFC provided the ability to study and understand the influences of water recovery
on-orbit if an ISS BPA processed PTU versus the nominal UPA-brine. The test series discussed confirmed that
bladders can be used for processing PTU and that refilling is possible with expected decay in production rates as high
as 50% specially when processing UPA-brine post a PTU dewatering cycle. Pore wetting was a major indicator that
was manifested during the entire test campaign even with the elimination of interruptions and downtime within test
cycles. A brine leak sensor event occurred during the PTAU test series however no visible leak indications were found.
    As forward work the team wants to explore the use and reuse of bladders for UPA-brine dewatering cycles to
understand possibilities and expectations in case of an on-orbit emergency situation where bladders cannot be replaced
between cycles or just as a contingency plan for ground support due to the limited availability of flight bladders for
ground studies. Additionally, the team wants to explore alternative potential flush and cleaning processes between
dewatering cycles.
    Moreover, the MSFC Ground BPA Test Bed is also preparing additional test objectives and implementation of
targeted upgrades meant for future Exploration minding architectures. The ISS BPA project has already redesigned
the Detrusor for an improved brine leak sensor design, later flying for installation before end of ISS. Other lessons
learned within the ISS BPA operations can also be either addressed with ground testing or explored on this test bed.
To that end, when hardware becomes available, the Ground BPA would look to install latest designs for continued
reliability assessments outside of the ISS operational environment. Teams are also looking into completing feasibility
studies for variable fan control speeds as a means to encourage increased production rates throughout a dewatering
cycle.
